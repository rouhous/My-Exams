Listening 1 · A change of venue · 7 marks

Listen to the conversation and choose one answer for each question.

Teacher listening script

Maha: Did you see the crowd outside the library yesterday? Everyone thought our recording workshop was starting there. Nasser: I did. The booking was correct, but the poster still showed the room we used last term. Maha: Didn't we replace that poster when the science club asked for our room? Nasser: We changed the one beside reception. Someone had already photographed the original and shared it. Maha: That explains why my cousin showed me a message with the right time and the wrong room. Nasser: Exactly. I was going to delete the photograph, but I couldn't remove other people's copies. Maha: What finally helped the visitors who were already standing outside the library? Nasser: The librarian put a student by the door to walk them to the studio. Maha: Could we put the room number on every ticket next time? Nasser: Only if it stays the same. A ticket printed on Monday could be wrong by Thursday. Maha: Then let's give people one place where they can always find the latest details. Nasser: My uncle suggested using the school website. He checks it on his lunch break. Maha: Would that help the grandparents who come early and leave their phones at home? Nasser: Probably not. A board at reception would serve them better, provided somebody updates it. Maha: Who could take responsibility for both the website and the board? Nasser: I'll do the website. Could you change the board as soon as I call? Maha: Yes. I'll add the time of the change so visitors know the notice is current. Nasser: Good idea. What about a short test before the next workshop begins? Maha: Let's give three visitors the old photograph and see whether they can find the studio. Nasser: Should we tell them which entrance to use before they start? Maha: No. We need to see where they actually look for help. Nasser: I'll watch from reception and write down the first place each person checks. Maha: And if somebody still gets lost, we'll ask which sign they expected to see. Nasser: That should tell us where to put the next notice. Maha: Good. I'll bring the old photograph on Wednesday.

  1. Where did visitors first gather?
  2. How did the outdated information spread?
  3. Who arranged for someone to guide the visitors?
  4. Why is Nasser unsure about printing the room on tickets?
  5. What will Maha add to the board besides the new information?
  6. Why will the volunteers receive no directions before the test?
  7. What will Nasser record?

Listening 2 · A talking map · 8 marks

Listen to Part One and answer questions 1–4 briefly. Listen to Part Two and choose the answer for questions 5–8.

Teacher listening script

Part One When our youth centre asked me to explain how its talking map works, I brought along the first map we made. It looks beautiful on paper. Each building has a coloured dot beside it, and the paths curve neatly around the trees. Yet on our first walk a visitor stopped at the gate and asked which way to turn. A blue dot meant nothing to someone listening to a description through headphones. That question changed the way we wrote directions. We began each route at the ticket desk because everyone had to pass it. We asked volunteers to describe one short section at a time. Instead of saying that the garden was lovely, they named a sound or a surface a walker could recognise. For example, the gravel ends just before the path divides. We recorded the directions in the small office behind the hall. Curtains there softened the echo, so even quiet words were easy to hear. Before lunch we had made recordings for three paths. We kept the original files so that a change to one sentence would never mean recording a whole route again. Part Two The next stage was testing. Each volunteer walked with a partner who stayed silent unless there was a risk of injury. We wanted walkers to tell us when an instruction arrived too late. One person heard the words “turn left” after she had already passed the turning. Making the speaker talk faster did little to help. Moving the instruction to the previous recording gave the walker time to prepare. Our biggest surprise came on a windy afternoon. A gate that usually made a clicking sound was standing open, so nobody heard it. We replaced that clue with the change from stone to wood underfoot. We also added a pause button after a visitor asked for time to listen to the birds. Next month a new path will open beside the pond. Before we publish its recording, two visitors who have never used the map will try it independently. People who know the gardens well often fill in missing details without noticing. A newcomer can show us exactly which words we still need.

  1. Where did every recorded route begin?
  2. What surface ends before the path divides?
  3. Where did the team record the directions?
  4. What softened the echo?
  5. When could a walking partner speak?
  6. How did the team improve the late turning instruction?
  7. Why did the gate become an unreliable clue?
  8. Why will newcomers test the pond route?

Reading 1 · Short texts · 5 marks

Read each text and decide whether the statement is true or false.

  1. Tickets for Saturday’s match can be collected on Friday afternoon. Bring your booking number to the school office. If a friend collects your ticket, give them your number and a signed note. The office closes at four. — A friend needs only the booking number to collect a ticket.
  2. The bakery is trying a new delivery route this week. Bread will reach the hill village before the harbour shops. Customers in both places will pay the usual price, even if their order arrives earlier than expected. — The earlier delivery costs extra.
  3. After a month of practice, I could finally play the entire tune without looking at the notes. My little brother recorded it for our aunt. I nearly stopped at the last line, but he encouraged me to finish. — The writer finished playing the tune.
  4. The new water bottles have a narrow opening that prevents spills. Wash the lid by hand after each use. The body of the bottle can go in the dishwasher, provided you remove the paper label first. — The lid should be washed by hand.
  5. Our walking group left the beach just after sunrise. We planned to reach the tower by noon, but stopped to help a cyclist repair a wheel. We ate lunch beside the road and reached the tower later. — The group ate lunch at the tower.

Reading 2 · A listening table · 7 marks

When our library replaced its suggestion box with a weekly listening table, I expected the same four confident students to do all the talking. I was wrong about who would come, although the table still has a problem that its organisers need to solve.

The old box stood beside the exit. You could drop in a private note in seconds. Its weakness was the silence afterwards. When I asked for more books on local birds, I had no idea whether anyone had read my request. At the listening table, a librarian writes down your suggestion and explains what can happen next. One student requested more sockets for laptops. She discovered that an electrician would need to check the room first. Although she left without a new socket, she understood the delay.

Another strength is the chance to improve an idea together. A boy suggested buying ten copies of the same adventure story because his friends wanted to read it. After talking with another reader, he proposed a book exchange instead. It cost nothing and attracted readers with different tastes. I doubt that two separate notes in the box would have produced that result.

However, the table is open only during the lunch break, when some students attend clubs or help younger pupils. Counting visitors gives an impressive total but tells us little about the people who cannot come. A meeting held entirely in public also makes it hard to raise a sensitive concern. I watched one girl walk past twice before leaving without speaking.

I would keep the table and put a small box beside it. Each Friday the librarian could post a short reply to common suggestions without naming anyone. This would preserve the best part of the conversation, a visible response, while giving students a choice about when and how to contribute. The measure of success should be whose voices we hear, as well as how many.

  1. What did the writer initially expect?
  2. What frustrated the writer about the bird-book suggestion?
  3. Why did the writer include the socket request?
  4. How did the book exchange begin?
  5. Why is the visitor total a limited measure?
  6. What does the girl walking past illustrate?
  7. Which arrangement does the writer favour?

Reading 3 · The missing label · 8 marks

The evening before our school exhibition, I discovered that the most interesting object on my table had no label. It was a small wooden box with tiny holes in the lid. My grandfather had lent it to me, and I had written down his explanation on a yellow card. Now the card was missing. I emptied my bag twice and even looked inside the box. Nothing.

My first idea was to search the internet. I found a photograph of a box almost exactly like ours. The description called it a box for keeping fishing hooks dry. I wrote a new label, but something bothered me. Why would anyone make holes in a lid if the purpose was to keep water out? I put the label in my pocket and went to find my grandfather.

He was sitting outside repairing a chair. When I showed him the photograph, he turned my box over and pointed to two faint letters underneath. They were his younger sister's initials. As a child she had used the box to carry silkworms from a neighbour's house. The holes let air inside. He remembered her feeding the silkworms leaves every morning before she went to school. I had heard the story before, but this time I understood why the holes mattered.

I asked if I could record him telling it. He agreed, provided his sister could hear the recording first. We called her that evening. She laughed at one detail: it was their neighbour who had supplied the leaves, because the tree in their own garden was still too small. I added her correction and printed a fresh card.

The next morning a visitor spent several minutes looking at the box. Instead of asking its price or age, she asked how we knew what it had been used for. I showed her the initials and played the two voices together. When I packed up that afternoon, I found the yellow card caught in the fold of the tablecloth. For a moment I wished I had found it sooner. Then I read its single sentence and slipped it back into my bag. Losing it had given me a better exhibit.

  1. What colour was the missing card?
  2. What did the online description say the box was used to store?
  3. What was Grandfather repairing?
  4. Whose initials were under the box?
  5. Why did the narrator hesitate to use the online explanation?
  6. What condition did Grandfather set for recording?
  7. What did the sister correct?
  8. Why was losing the first card ultimately helpful?
